## What changes at a shop with one barber

Most men in Sherman Oaks already have a haircut option closer than this one. Usually several. The question is not whether a closer chair exists — it is what the closer chair does differently, and the honest answer has nothing to do with skill and everything to do with structure.

At a shop with a rota, you book a haircut. You do not book a person. Whoever is free takes you, and the man who cut your hair well in March is not necessarily the man who cuts it in July. That means the growth pattern conversation resets every visit. Your cowlick gets rediscovered. The neckline decision gets made again, sometimes differently, and a neckline that has been blocked once and tapered the next time never quite settles.

Here there is one barber. Amir cuts every appointment, and the chair is not double-booked, which changes two things. The first is continuity: the second visit starts where the first one ended rather than at the beginning. The second is pace — nobody is being started in your slot, so the last five minutes of the cut are not being borrowed from someone else's first five.

> "The second haircut is where a client finds out whether it was luck. If it is the same hands, it is not luck."
> — Amir, barber

None of that is an argument against the shop down the street from you. It is a description of a trade: a few more minutes in the car for a chair that remembers what your hair does.

## Whether it is worth the ten minutes

That depends on something specific: how your last three haircuts compared to each other.

If they were the same cut three times, whatever you are doing is working, and there is no argument here worth reading. If they were three variations — one good, one flat, one where the back was suddenly higher than you wanted — then the inconsistency is structural rather than bad luck, and driving ten minutes west along the boulevard is a cheap way to fix a structural problem.

Men from Sherman Oaks, Van Nuys and Studio City come in for that reason more than any other. Not because Encino has better barbers — there is no argument being made about neighbourhoods here. Because one chair produces one result, and three visits to one chair produce three versions of the same haircut rather than three different haircuts.
